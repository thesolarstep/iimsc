Section 01

What Is Mass Spectrometry?

Mass spectrometry (MS) is an analytical technique that measures the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of ions. By doing so, it provides precise information about the molecular weight, structure, and identity of compounds — from small metabolites to entire viral proteomes.

At its core, every mass spectrometer does three things: ionises analyte molecules, separates ions by m/z, and detects them to generate a spectrum.

The Fundamental Principle

All mass analysers exploit the fact that ions with different m/z ratios behave differently in electromagnetic fields. The m/z value is calculated as:

m/z = (molecular mass + z × 1.00728) / z

where z is the number of charges (protons gained or lost). A singly charged ion (z=1) of glucose (MW ≈ 180 Da) appears at m/z 181 (adding one proton).

🔑 Key Concept

MS does not measure mass directly — it measures the ratio of mass to charge. A doubly charged ion appears at half the m/z of a singly charged ion of the same molecule. This is crucial when interpreting spectra of multiply charged proteins.

A Brief History

J.J. Thomson built the first mass spectrograph in 1912 to study isotopes of neon — demonstrating that neon was actually two isotopes (20Ne and 22Ne). Francis Aston later won the 1922 Nobel Prize for mapping the isotopes of most elements. For decades MS remained a physicist's tool for studying small atoms and gases.

Historical Anecdote

The leap into biology came dramatically in 1989 when John Fenn and Koichi Tanaka (separately) developed techniques to ionise intact proteins without destroying them — ESI and MALDI respectively. Fenn famously said his technique could "weigh an elephant one hair at a time." Both received (or should have received) Nobel Prizes: Fenn won in 2002; Tanaka — a production engineer with no PhD — was the surprise co-winner. He was reportedly so shocked by the announcement he assumed a colleague was pranking him.

Section 02

Ionisation Methods

Ionisation is arguably the most critical step — a poor ionisation method destroys or obscures your analyte before it ever reaches the analyser. The choice depends on molecule size, volatility, and whether intact molecular ions are needed.

Electrospray Ionisation (ESI)

ESI is the workhorse of biological MS. A liquid sample is pumped through a metal capillary at high voltage (~3–5 kV). The strong electric field sprays the liquid into a fine mist of charged droplets. As solvent evaporates, the droplets shrink until ions are expelled into the gas phase.

ESI produces multiply charged ions from large biomolecules — a protein of 50 kDa might appear as a series of peaks from [M+20H]20+ to [M+30H]30+, all within the typical m/z 500–2000 range of most instruments.

🔑 Why Multiply Charged Ions Matter

A 150 kDa antibody would require an analyser with m/z range up to 150,000 if it only carried one charge. ESI gives it ~50–60 charges, compressing it to m/z ~2,500–3,000 — perfectly measurable on standard instruments.

MALDI — Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionisation

In MALDI, the analyte is co-crystallised with a UV-absorbing matrix compound (e.g., α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid, CHCA; or sinapinic acid). A pulsed laser fires at the crystal; the matrix absorbs energy, vaporises, and carries analyte ions into the gas phase — largely singly charged.

Because MALDI produces predominantly singly charged [M+H]⁺ ions, spectra are simpler than ESI. MALDI-TOF (paired with a time-of-flight analyser) is the dominant technology for rapid microbial identification in clinical and food microbiology labs — more on this in Section 6.

Electron Ionisation (EI) & Chemical Ionisation (CI)

EI is the classic method for small, volatile, non-polar molecules (e.g., fatty acid methyl esters, VOCs). A beam of 70 eV electrons bombards vaporised analyte, causing extensive fragmentation. This fragmentation is reproducible, making EI spectra matchable against the NIST library of >350,000 compounds — invaluable for microbial metabolite profiling.

CI uses a reagent gas (methane, ammonia) to produce softer ionisation with less fragmentation, giving a prominent molecular ion useful when EI shatters the molecule beyond recognition.

Lab Anecdote

EI was used in the 1960s to identify amino acids in the Murchison meteorite that fell in Australia in 1969 — some of them not present in Earth organisms. The reproducibility of 70 eV fragmentation patterns meant spectra taken on Earth could be compared decades later with modern databases. MS's ability to identify molecules from tiny samples without needing a "reference strain" parallels its power in clinical microbiology today.

Section 03

Mass Analysers

After ionisation, ions are accelerated into the mass analyser — the "heart" of the instrument that physically separates them by m/z. Different analysers offer different trade-offs of resolution, mass accuracy, scan speed, and cost.

Quadrupole (Q)

Four parallel metal rods carry alternating RF and DC voltages. Only ions of a specific m/z have stable trajectories and pass through; others collide with the rods. By scanning the voltages, a mass spectrum is built sequentially.

  • Resolution: unit (1 Da)
  • Mass accuracy: ~100–200 ppm
  • Strengths: robust, fast, excellent for targeted quantification (MRM/SRM)
  • Weaknesses: limited mass range (typically <3,000 m/z), low resolution

Time-of-Flight (TOF)

Ions are accelerated by the same electric potential, giving all ions the same kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²). Lighter ions therefore travel faster down a flight tube and arrive at the detector sooner. The time of flight is proportional to √(m/z).

t = L × √(m/z × 1/(2eV))

where L is tube length, V is accelerating voltage, and e is elementary charge. A reflectron (ion mirror) corrects for small kinetic energy spreads, greatly improving resolution.

  • Resolution: 10,000–40,000 (reflectron)
  • Mass accuracy: 5–20 ppm (with calibration)
  • Unlimited mass range in principle
  • Essential for MALDI-TOF microbial ID and intact protein analysis

Orbitrap & Ion Trap

The Orbitrap (invented by Alexander Makarov in 1999, commercialised by Thermo Scientific) traps ions in oscillating orbits around a spindle electrode. Ions of different m/z oscillate at characteristic frequencies; Fourier transform converts the image current into a mass spectrum.

  • Resolution: up to 500,000 (FWHM) — distinguishes 13C isotopes from isobaric peaks
  • Mass accuracy: <1 ppm (with internal calibration)
  • The gold standard for high-resolution discovery proteomics and metabolomics
Instrument Anecdote

The Orbitrap was born from Makarov's PhD thesis work on ion kinetics in the USSR. When he joined what became Thermo Scientific, he spent years building an instrument his colleagues doubted would ever work. The first commercial Orbitrap (LTQ-Orbitrap) launched in 2005 and within five years had revolutionised proteomics, enabling identification of >10,000 proteins in a single experiment — something that had taken entire PhD projects with earlier instruments.

Section 04

Tandem Mass Spectrometry (MS/MS)

A single MS experiment tells you the mass of a molecule. Tandem MS (MS/MS or MS²) goes further: it selects a specific ion from the first MS, fragments it, then analyses the fragments in a second MS stage. This yields structural information — the identity of amino acid sequences, lipid backbones, glycan structures, etc.

Dissociation Methods

Collision-Induced Dissociation (CID / HCD) ▼

The precursor ion is accelerated into an inert collision gas (nitrogen or argon). Kinetic energy converts to vibrational energy, breaking the weakest bonds. In peptides, CID preferentially cleaves the peptide bond to give b and y ions — the cornerstone of bottom-up proteomics. HCD (higher-energy collisional dissociation) in Orbitrap instruments gives high-resolution fragment spectra.

Electron Transfer Dissociation (ETD) & ECD ▼

Radical electrons are transferred to multiply protonated peptides, causing N–Cα backbone cleavage to give c and z ions. ETD is gentler than CID and preserves labile modifications such as phosphorylation and glycosylation — invaluable for post-translational modification (PTM) mapping. It works best on highly charged (z ≥ 3) ions.

In-Source Fragmentation & MSⁿ ▼

Ion traps can perform MS³, MS⁴, etc. (MSⁿ) by isolating a fragment ion and fragmenting it again. This allows complete structural elucidation of glycans and complex lipids. While powerful, sensitivity drops with each stage due to ion losses.

Ion Series in Peptide Fragmentation

When a peptide is fragmented by CID, the nomenclature follows the Roepstorff–Fohlman system: ions retaining the N-terminus are labelled a, b, c; ions retaining the C-terminus are x, y, z. The most common are b and y ions.

b₁ b₂ b₃ … [peptide] … y₃ y₂ y₁

A series of consecutive y ions differing by the mass of one amino acid residue allows direct de novo sequencing of the peptide.

🔑 Shotgun Proteomics Workflow

In bottom-up shotgun proteomics: proteins are digested with trypsin (cleaves after Lys/Arg), peptides are separated by LC, ionised by ESI, and subjected to MS/MS (DDA or DIA). Fragment spectra are matched against protein databases (Mascot, SEQUEST, MaxQuant) to identify proteins and quantify abundance.

Section 05

Detectors & Data Output

Ions leaving the analyser must be detected and converted to an electrical signal. The most common detector is the electron multiplier: an ion strikes a conversion dynode, releasing secondary electrons that cascade through a series of dynodes, amplifying the signal by 10⁶–10⁸ fold.

Modern instruments output raw data as an m/z spectrum (x-axis: m/z; y-axis: relative ion abundance). For LC-MS/MS experiments, a Total Ion Chromatogram (TIC) shows total ion current over elution time, while Extracted Ion Chromatograms (XICs) trace specific m/z values — used for quantification.

Key Metrics

  • Resolution (R): R = m/Δm — the ability to distinguish two closely spaced peaks
  • Mass accuracy: difference between measured and theoretical mass (ppm)
  • Dynamic range: ratio of the most to least abundant detectable ion (typically 10³–10⁵)
  • Sensitivity: minimum detectable amount (often fmol–amol for modern instruments)
⚠ Common Pitfall

Students often confuse resolution and mass accuracy. A low-resolution instrument can have good mass accuracy (it reads the correct mass, just can't separate nearby peaks). An Orbitrap has both — but a quadrupole in triple-quad mode has moderate accuracy and low resolution, relying on fragmentation pattern for specificity, not mass alone.

Section 06

Microbial Identification by MALDI-TOF MS

MALDI-TOF MS has transformed clinical microbiology since the mid-2000s. Traditional biochemical identification (API strips, Vitek) required 24–48 h; MALDI-TOF delivers a species-level identification in minutes from a single colony.

How It Works

A colony is transferred to a steel target plate, overlaid with MALDI matrix (typically CHCA or DHB), and allowed to air-dry. The laser fires, vaporising predominantly ribosomal proteins (the most abundant and conserved proteins in bacteria, 2–20 kDa range). The resulting mass fingerprint is compared against a database of reference spectra.

🔑 Why Ribosomal Proteins?

Ribosomal proteins are (1) highly abundant — constituting ~25% of dry cell mass, (2) evolutionarily conserved in sequence, giving species-specific mass patterns, and (3) soluble and small enough for MALDI. The pattern of peaks between 2–12 kDa acts as a "spectral fingerprint."

Limitations & Challenges

  • Database gaps: rare organisms, non-culturable species, and novel pathogens may not match. A new Elizabethkingia outbreak strain may score poorly if the database strain differs significantly.
  • Strain-level resolution: Standard MALDI-TOF reliably identifies to species level, but strain typing (e.g., distinguishing MRSA strains) requires additional tools. Peak pattern analysis at higher mass ranges or direct-on-plate typing algorithms are active research areas.
  • Blood culture bottles: Direct identification from positive blood cultures requires a short extraction step to remove blood proteins that suppress ionisation.
  • Anaerobes and moulds: Require more rigorous extraction (ethanol/formic acid). Fungal ID is improving but still less reliable than bacterial.
  • Antibiotic resistance: Cannot reliably detect resistance by standard MALDI-TOF — requires dedicated assays (see Section 10).
Clinical Anecdote

In a landmark 2010 study at the Charité hospital in Berlin, MALDI-TOF was deployed for routine bacteriology identification. The total cost per identification dropped from €24.0 (conventional) to €1.3. More importantly, time-to-identification dropped from >24 h to under 10 minutes for sub-cultured colonies. For bloodstream infections, every hour of delay in appropriate antibiotic therapy increases mortality by ~7% — making these time savings clinically meaningful. Today, MALDI-TOF is the standard of care in most major clinical microbiology labs worldwide.

Section 07

Proteomics & Metaproteomics

Proteomics is the large-scale study of all proteins expressed by a cell or organism at a given time — the proteome. Unlike the static genome, the proteome is dynamic, reflecting the organism's actual metabolic state.

Bottom-Up vs. Top-Down Proteomics

Bottom-Up (Shotgun) Proteomics ▼

Proteins are first digested into peptides (usually by trypsin), separated by reversed-phase LC, and analysed by ESI-MS/MS. Peptide spectra are matched against protein databases. This is the most common approach, capable of identifying and quantifying thousands of proteins per sample. Drawback: protein inference ambiguity (different proteins may share peptides) and loss of intact protein information.

Top-Down Proteomics ▼

Intact proteins are injected directly (no digestion), separated by LC or capillary electrophoresis, and fragmented in the MS. High-resolution instruments (FT-ICR, Orbitrap) are required. Top-down preserves all PTMs in context — valuable for studying proteoforms (e.g., alternative splicing, multiple phosphorylations). Technically challenging due to complex spectra and lower sensitivity for large proteins.

Metaproteomics

Metaproteomics applies shotgun proteomics to complex microbial communities — gut microbiome, soil, biofilms, ocean samples. The combined protein extract from thousands of species is digested and analysed by LC-MS/MS. Spectra are matched against metagenome-derived sequence databases.

Key questions addressed by metaproteomics:

  • Which species are metabolically active (not just present — DNA can linger from dead cells)?
  • What functions are being performed — who is breaking down cellulose, fixing nitrogen, producing short-chain fatty acids?
  • How does community function change in disease (e.g., IBD gut microbiome vs. healthy)?
🔑 Metaproteomics vs. Metagenomics

Metagenomics tells you who is there and what they could do. Metaproteomics tells you what they are actually doing right now. Both together (meta-omics integration) provide the most complete picture of microbiome function.

Quantification Strategies

  • Label-free quantification (LFQ): compare XIC peak areas across runs. Simple but requires careful normalisation.
  • SILAC (Stable Isotope Labelling by Amino acids in Cell culture): cells grown in ¹³C/¹⁵N amino acids. Pairs of heavy/light peptides elute together; ratio = fold-change. Gold standard for cell culture proteomics.
  • TMT/iTRAQ (isobaric mass tags): up to 18 samples multiplexed in one run. Tags add equal mass to all samples but release diagnostic reporter ions in MS/MS. Widely used for clinical cohort studies.
  • DIA (Data-Independent Acquisition): all precursor ions are fragmented systematically (no selection). Results in highly reproducible quantification; analysed by Spectronaut or DIA-NN software.
Section 08

Metabolomics

The metabolome comprises all small molecules (<1,500 Da) in a biological sample — amino acids, organic acids, nucleotides, lipids, xenobiotics. It is the closest readout to phenotype: the integrated output of genome + transcriptome + proteome + environment.

Untargeted vs. Targeted Metabolomics

Untargeted (discovery): Measure as many metabolites as possible without prior hypothesis. Use HRMS (Orbitrap, QTOF). Yields thousands of features; unknown metabolites are a major challenge — <40% of features can typically be annotated even in well-studied organisms.

Targeted (quantitative): Pre-selected metabolites measured by triple quadrupole MRM. Highly sensitive, accurate, and reproducible — used for clinical biomarker validation.

MS in Microbial Metabolomics

  • Secondary metabolite discovery: fungi and actinobacteria are prolific producers of bioactive compounds. MS-guided fractionation (GNPS molecular networking) identifies novel antibiotics, mycotoxins, and signalling molecules.
  • Quorum sensing molecules: N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs) in gram-negatives are detected by LC-MS/MS at nM concentrations.
  • Volatile organic compounds (VOCs): headspace SPME–GC-EI-MS detects diagnostic VOC profiles from bacterial cultures — being evaluated for breath-based infection diagnostics.
  • Isotope tracing: ¹³C-labelled substrates fed to bacteria; mass shifts of metabolites reveal active pathway flux (metabolic flux analysis).
Research Anecdote

In 2012, researchers at the Scripps Institution used GNPS (Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking) — a crowd-sourced MS/MS database — to discover that a marine actinobacterium made a novel compound related to a known antibiotic. The compound had been detected before but misidentified because conventional chromatography doesn't separate closely related analogues. MS/MS networking grouped the spectra by structural similarity, revealing the family relationship. This approach has since been used to discover dozens of novel bioactive microbial metabolites.

Section 09

Lipidomics & Lipopolysaccharides

Bacterial lipids are crucial virulence factors, membrane components, and diagnostic markers. MS has become indispensable for lipid characterisation.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) Analysis

LPS (endotoxin) of Gram-negative bacteria consists of Lipid A (the toxic component, embedded in the outer membrane), a core oligosaccharide, and the O-antigen polysaccharide. MS analysis of LPS provides:

  • Lipid A fatty acid composition — key for understanding TLR4 agonism/antagonism
  • Core sugar modifications that correlate with antibiotic resistance (e.g., phosphoethanolamine additions in colistin resistance)
  • O-antigen structure for serotyping E. coli, Salmonella, and other pathogens

Lipid A is typically analysed by negative-mode ESI-MS or MALDI-TOF after isolation by Bligh–Dyer extraction. Its high negative charge density makes negative-mode ionisation efficient.

🔑 Colistin Resistance & MS

Resistance to colistin (last-resort antibiotic) involves addition of phosphoethanolamine (PEtN) or 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose (Ara4N) to Lipid A, reducing negative charge and colistin binding. These modifications cause a mass shift of +123 Da (PEtN) or +131 Da (Ara4N) detectable by MS — allowing direct resistance mechanism identification from lipid extracts.

Intact Lipid Analysis

Shotgun lipidomics (direct infusion ESI-MS/MS) or LC-MS/MS can profile thousands of lipid species from microbial membranes. This has revealed, for example, that Mycobacterium tuberculosis uses unique lipids (mycocerosic acids, phthiocerol dimycocerosates) as virulence factors — structures only fully elucidated by tandem MS.

Section 10

Antibiotic Resistance Detection by MS

Conventional AST (Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing) — disk diffusion, MIC broth dilution — requires 16–24 h of growth. MS-based approaches aim to dramatically shorten this.

β-Lactamase Detection (MALDI-TOF)

Bacteria are incubated with a β-lactam antibiotic (e.g., meropenem) for 2–4 hours. If the organism produces β-lactamase, the antibiotic is hydrolysed — detectable as a mass shift of +18 Da (water addition to the β-lactam ring). MALDI-TOF directly measures the intact drug and its hydrolysis product.

Meropenem (MW 383 Da) → Hydrolysed meropenem (MW 401 Da)

This approach successfully detects carbapenemases (KPC, NDM, OXA) within 2–4 hours — faster than any culture-based method. Commercial kits (MBT STAR-BL, Bruker) are now available.

Whole-Cell Resistance Markers

MALDI-TOF spectra can distinguish MRSA from MSSA in some settings by detecting the presence/absence of a specific peak (the PBP2a protein at ~78 kDa is outside the standard 2–12 kDa window, but modified analysis conditions can detect it). More practically, subtype-specific peaks in ribosomal spectra correlate with certain resistance genotypes in organisms like Candida auris.

Emerging: Isotope Ratio MS for AMR

By using ¹³C-labelled glucose and measuring isotopic incorporation into metabolites, it is possible to assess bacterial metabolic activity in the presence of antibiotics within 30–60 minutes — an entire culture cycle shorter than standard AST. This approach is being developed for rapid phenotypic AST in research settings.

Clinical Anecdote

In 2019, a multi-centre European study showed that MALDI-TOF-based carbapenemase detection from positive blood cultures reduced the time from culture positivity to actionable resistance result from >24 h to ~3 h. In one simulated patient scenario, this translated to the difference between appropriate carbapenem-sparing therapy and a full day of empiric carbapenem exposure — with implications for both patient outcomes and antimicrobial stewardship.

Section 11

Data Analysis & Bioinformatics

Raw MS data is meaningless without appropriate processing. The challenge scales enormously with experiment type — a MALDI-TOF identification is automated in seconds; a shotgun metaproteomics dataset may contain millions of spectra requiring days of computational analysis.

False Discovery Rate (FDR) Control

With millions of spectra, random chance matches accumulate. The target-decoy strategy is standard: a reversed (decoy) protein database is appended to the real database. Matches to decoys estimate the false positive rate. Filtering at 1% FDR means ~1% of reported peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) are incorrect.

⚠ FDR Caution

1% PSM-level FDR does not mean 1% protein-level FDR. A protein identified by only one peptide is much less reliable than one with many peptides. Always apply protein-level FDR separately and prefer proteins identified by ≥2 unique peptides.

Section 12

Glossary & Flashcards

Key Terminology

m/z
Mass-to-charge ratio. The x-axis of a mass spectrum. Determines where an ion appears in the spectrum.
Dalton (Da)
Unit of molecular mass; 1 Da = 1/12 the mass of a ¹²C atom (~1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg).
Precursor ion
The ion selected in MS1 for fragmentation in MS/MS. Also called parent ion.
Product ion
A fragment ion produced from the precursor ion during MS/MS. Also called daughter ion.
Monoisotopic mass
Mass calculated using the lightest isotope of each element (¹H, ¹²C, ¹⁴N, etc.). Used in database searches.
Average mass
Mass calculated using natural isotope abundances. Relevant for low-resolution measurements of larger molecules.
Trypsin digestion
Proteolytic cleavage at C-terminal of Lys (K) and Arg (R). Most commonly used enzyme in bottom-up proteomics.
MRM / SRM
Multiple/Selected Reaction Monitoring. Targeted quantification using precursor → product ion transitions on a triple quadrupole.
DDA
Data-Dependent Acquisition. Instrument selects the most abundant precursors for MS/MS in real time.
DIA
Data-Independent Acquisition. All precursors in m/z windows are fragmented systematically. More reproducible quantification.
MALDI score
Bruker Biotyper log(score): ≥2.0 = species ID; 1.7–2.0 = genus probable; <1.7 = unreliable match.
Reflectron
Ion mirror in a TOF instrument that corrects kinetic energy spread, improving resolution.
In-source fragmentation
Unintended fragmentation in the ion source (before the mass analyser). Can complicate spectra.
Matrix suppression
MALDI matrix helps prevent ion suppression from salts and non-volatile contaminants.
Isotope pattern
Cluster of peaks at M, M+1, M+2 from natural isotope abundances. Used to determine charge state and verify molecular formula.
Charge deconvolution
Computational process that converts multiply charged ESI ion series back into a neutral mass spectrum.
