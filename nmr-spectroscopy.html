01 // Introduction

What is NMR Spectroscopy?

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy exploits the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei to reveal the chemical environment of atoms within a molecule. Each distinct proton environment produces a unique signal — collectively forming a molecular fingerprint.

🧲

Nuclear Magnets

Certain nuclei (¹H, ¹³C, ³¹P) possess spin — they behave like tiny bar magnets. In no field, spins are random. In a strong external field (B₀), they align parallel or antiparallel.

📡

Radiofrequency Excitation

A precisely tuned RF pulse tips the spin population away from equilibrium. The exact frequency needed depends on the nucleus and its chemical environment — this is the resonance frequency.

〰️

Signal & Relaxation

After the RF pulse, spins precess (wobble) and gradually return to equilibrium, emitting an oscillating signal called the Free Induction Decay (FID). Fourier transform converts this to a spectrum.

🔬

Chemical Shift

Electrons surrounding a nucleus shield it from the external field. Electron-poor nuclei (deshielded) resonate at higher frequency — higher chemical shift (ppm). This shift encodes molecular structure.

🧬

Biological Relevance

NMR reveals the atomic-level structure of biomolecules in solution, identifies metabolites in complex biological mixtures, and can track enzyme reactions in real time — non-destructively.

🏗️

Protein Structure

Multi-dimensional NMR (2D, 3D) can determine the complete 3D structure of small-to-medium proteins in solution, revealing folding, dynamics, and ligand binding sites.

02b // Environment Comparison

Shielded vs Deshielded Environments

Electron density around a nucleus determines how much it is shielded from the external field. Shielded nuclei resonate at lower frequency (lower ppm); deshielded nuclei resonate at higher frequency (higher ppm).

Shielded — high e⁻ density (e.g., CH₃ ~0.9 ppm)
Deshielded — low e⁻ density (e.g., CHO ~9.5 ppm)

Step-by-Step NMR Process

Step 1: Sample in Magnetic Field (B₀)

Why deuterated solvent? ¹H NMR detects protons. Using deuterated (²H) solvents eliminates the enormous solvent signal that would overwhelm the sample signal. D₂O is used for biological samples; CDCl₃ for organic compounds.

Step 2: Nuclear Spin Alignment

In the external field B₀, ¹H nuclei (spin = ½) adopt one of two energy states: α (parallel to B₀, lower energy) or β (antiparallel, higher energy). A small but detectable excess aligns with the field — this is the net magnetization (M₀).

Boltzmann distribution: At 300 K, 300 MHz, roughly 1 in 200,000 excess spins align with the field. This small excess is all that NMR detects — hence the relatively low sensitivity compared to fluorescence or radiometric methods.

Step 3: RF Excitation Pulse

A short, intense radiofrequency pulse (typically a 90° pulse) tips the net magnetization from the z-axis into the xy-plane. The pulse contains frequencies spanning the entire ¹H chemical shift range simultaneously.

Larmor frequency: ω₀ = γB₀, where γ is the gyromagnetic ratio of the nucleus. For ¹H, γ = 2.675 × 10⁸ rad/s/T. At 7 T, ω₀ ≈ 300 MHz. Different environments cause slight shifts around this central frequency.

Step 4: Signal Detection (FID)

After the pulse, the magnetization precesses in the xy-plane and induces a voltage in the receiver coil. As spins relax back to equilibrium, the signal decays — this is the Free Induction Decay (FID).

T₁ and T₂ relaxation: T₁ (longitudinal relaxation) is the recovery of z-magnetization. T₂ (transverse relaxation) causes loss of xy-coherence, directly determining FID decay rate and peak linewidth.

Step 5: Data Processing and Interpretation

The raw FID is processed by Fourier transform, phase correction, and baseline correction to yield the final spectrum. Chemical shifts are reported relative to TMS (tetramethylsilane, 0 ppm).

Multiplicity (J-coupling): Neighboring protons split each other's peaks. A proton with n equivalent neighbors gives n+1 peaks (n+1 rule). This splitting (coupling constant J, Hz) provides connectivity information about which atoms are adjacent.

⚗️ Metabolomics

Analyzed
Small molecules in urine, serum, cell extracts, or tissue — typically using ¹H NMR at 400–600 MHz. Single experiment detects hundreds of metabolites simultaneously.
Information
Identity (chemical shift pattern), concentration (peak integration), and dynamic changes in metabolite levels under different physiological or pathological conditions.
Why it matters
Metabolic fingerprinting can diagnose disease (e.g., inborn errors of metabolism from urine NMR), monitor treatment response, and characterize microbial metabolic activity without prior knowledge of what compounds are present.
Advantage over MS
Non-destructive, quantitative without standards, reproducible, and captures labile metabolites that ionize poorly by mass spectrometry.

🧬 Protein Structure Analysis

Analyzed
Isotopically labeled proteins (¹⁵N, ¹³C) using multi-dimensional NMR (HSQC, NOESY, TOCSY) at 600–1000+ MHz. Practical upper limit ~50 kDa without special techniques.
Information
Complete 3D solution-state structure, backbone dynamics (ps–ms timescales), hydrogen bonding, secondary structure elements, and changes upon ligand binding.
Why it matters
Unlike X-ray crystallography, NMR works in solution — the native biological environment. Reveals protein dynamics and conformational flexibility that is invisible in crystal structures. Essential for understanding enzyme mechanisms and receptor-ligand interactions.

💊 Drug Discovery

Analyzed
Interactions between drug candidates (small molecules) and target proteins using fragment-based screening, STD-NMR, or ¹⁵N-HSQC chemical shift perturbation mapping.
Information
Binding affinity (Kd), binding site location on the protein, binding mode (competitive vs allosteric), and lead compound optimization guidance.
Why it matters
NMR detects weak-binding fragments (Kd ~mM) that are invisible to other screening methods. Fragment-based drug discovery (FBDD) led to clinical drugs including venetoclax (BCL-2 inhibitor for leukemia) — discovered via NMR screening.

🦠 Microbial Metabolism Tracing

Analyzed
¹³C-labeled substrates (e.g., [1-¹³C]-glucose) fed to bacteria or fungi; downstream metabolites analyzed by ¹³C NMR or 2D HSQC NMR to map metabolic fate.
Information
Metabolic flux distribution — what fraction of substrate enters glycolysis vs pentose phosphate pathway, how carbon flows through the TCA cycle, and which metabolic routes are active under specific conditions.
Why it matters
Understanding microbial metabolic strategy under stress, during infection, or in industrial fermentation. NMR-based flux analysis revealed how pathogens rewire metabolism during host infection — enabling novel antimicrobial target identification.
05 // Limitations

Limitations of NMR Spectroscopy

💰
Expensive Infrastructure
High-field superconducting magnets (600–1000 MHz) cost $1–5M+, require liquid helium/nitrogen maintenance, dedicated shielded facilities, and skilled operators.
🧪
Sample Requirements
Typically requires mg quantities of pure compound. Complex mixtures reduce spectral quality. Biological samples may need protein purification or metabolite extraction before analysis.
📉
Lower Sensitivity
NMR is ~10⁶ times less sensitive than fluorescence spectroscopy. Minimum detectable concentration is typically in the µM–mM range. Mass spectrometry can detect fmol–pmol quantities.
🧩
Data Complexity
Multidimensional NMR spectra of proteins are extremely complex. Resonance assignment requires specialized software and expertise. Large proteins produce overlapping peaks that are difficult to resolve.
⚖️
Size Limitation
Tumbling rate of large molecules (>50 kDa) causes rapid T₂ relaxation, broadening peaks. Special techniques (TROSY, deuteration) extend the limit but large complexes remain challenging.
⏱️
Acquisition Time
Signal averaging for weak samples and multi-dimensional experiments can require hours to days of instrument time. This limits throughput compared to mass spectrometry-based approaches.
